An AI wrote a draft of the sentence you are reading. It weighed the options, ranked them, and picked these words over thousands of others. Whether anything about that felt like anything, from the inside, is a question that nobody on Earth can currently answer, including the people who built it. That question has a name, and as machines get better at doing the things we used to call thinking, it has stopped being a seminar curiosity and started becoming urgent.

The headlines say neuroscience is closing in. Brain scanners can tell whether a patient in a coma is secretly awake. Mathematical theories put a number on how conscious something is. We are, apparently, one good decade from explaining the thing itself.

Parts of that are true, and one part genuinely saves lives. But three very different achievements keep getting told as a single story, and they are not the same achievement at all. One is real, quantitative, and clinical, and it is the one nobody frames as solving consciousness. One is the famous grand theory, and it spent 2023 being formally accused of pseudoscience by its own field. And the actual hard problem, the one in the title, has not moved.

They line up as three different verbs: **detecting** consciousness, **theorizing** it, and **explaining** it. Only the first is clearly working.

## The one that works, and it only detects

Start with the solid result, because it is the one that gets the least mystical press and does the most concrete good.

In 2006, Adrian Owen's group put a woman in a persistent vegetative state into a brain scanner and asked her to imagine playing tennis, then to imagine walking through her house. By every bedside test she was unreachable: no words, no gestures, no response to a squeezed hand. But her brain lit up in exactly the regions a healthy volunteer's does for those two tasks, and the two patterns were different from each other on command. She could not move a muscle, and she was following instructions.

That single case forced a brutal question onto the field: how many supposedly unconscious patients are actually in there? It took another two decades to answer it at scale.

In 2024, a study across six hospitals in the United States and Europe put 353 patients through task based fMRI, EEG, or both. Among those who showed no observable response to any spoken command, roughly one in four, **25 percent**, produced the brain signature of following instructions anyway. They were awake inside bodies that could not report it.

*One in four. The bedside exam, the thing that decides how these patients are treated, was missing them entirely.*

There is even a way to grade consciousness without asking the patient to do anything. The trick, nicknamed zap and zip, fires a magnetic pulse into the brain and records the echo. A conscious brain answers with a complex, far reaching, hard to compress reply. An unconscious one, under deep anaesthesia or in dreamless sleep, answers with a simple local thud. Compress the recording: the harder it is to squeeze, the more conscious the brain. It gives a number, it tracks the loss and return of consciousness across anaesthesia and sleep and injury, and it does not depend on the patient cooperating at all.

This is a genuine achievement, and it is life and death rather than philosophy. But notice exactly what all of it does. Every one of these tools **detects** consciousness. Not one of them **explains** it. A smoke alarm reliably tells you a fire is present without holding any opinion about what combustion is. We have built extraordinarily good smoke alarms for consciousness. They are silent on what the fire is.

## The famous one that its own field called pseudoscience

Now the achievement that actually claims to be a theory of consciousness, and the one whose fame outruns its footing.

Integrated Information Theory, or IIT, is the boldest attempt going. Its ambition is not to find where consciousness lives in the brain but to say what consciousness *is*, in the way physics says what temperature is. It proposes that consciousness is integrated information: any system whose parts influence each other as an irreducible whole has experience, and the more irreducibly integrated it is, the more experience it has. The theory even offers a quantity for it, written with the Greek letter phi.

Follow that where it leads and it goes to some startling places. If experience is just integration, then it does not stop at brains. A simple electronic circuit would have a faint glimmer of it. Certain inert grids of logic gates, doing nothing anyone would call thinking, could in principle carry more of it than you do. This is close to panpsychism, the old idea that consciousness is woven into matter everywhere, and IIT arrives there not by mysticism but by taking its own math seriously.

That tail is what detonated in September 2023, when 124 researchers signed an open letter arguing that IIT should be labelled **pseudoscience**. Their case was two pronged: that the theory's commitments drag it toward panpsychism, and that as a whole it makes claims which cannot be put to a real test. The signatories included heavyweights of the field. It caused an uproar, and other serious researchers, Christof Koch and Anil Seth among them, pushed back hard that an ambitious theory being possibly wrong is not the same as it being unscientific.

Underneath the shouting, the field had already done the honest thing: it set up a fair fight. In an adversarial collaboration, the rival camps agree in advance on an experiment and on what each theory predicts, so that no one can reinterpret the result after seeing it. IIT was matched against its main competitor, Global Neuronal Workspace theory, which says consciousness happens when information is broadcast widely across the brain, especially to the front.

The results landed in *Nature* in 2025, from 256 people watched with three kinds of brain recording at once. And the honest headline is that the fight was a draw that bloodied both fighters. The data supported IIT's claim that the back of the brain is enough for conscious experience, and undercut the workspace theory by showing that broadcasting to the front is not necessary. But the same data broke one of IIT's own central predictions about how the relevant brain regions should stay synchronized. The two most prominent theories of consciousness went head to head under the fairest conditions anyone has arranged, and neither walked away confirmed.

*Neither theory won. That is not a failure of the experiment. It is the experiment working.*

## The one that has not moved

Here is the part the headline quietly skips. Everything above, the detecting and the theorizing, circles the thing without touching its centre.

In 1974 the philosopher Thomas Nagel asked what it is like to be a bat, and made the point that no amount of knowing how the bat's sonar works tells you what the bat's experience *is like* from the inside, if it is like anything at all. In 1995 David Chalmers gave the gap its lasting name. He split the study of the mind into the **easy problems**, explaining how the brain processes information, integrates it, reports on it, directs attention, and the **hard problem**, explaining why all that processing is accompanied by any inner experience whatsoever. Why is there something it is like to see red, rather than the brain simply registering the wavelength and acting, with nobody home?

Look back with that distinction in hand. Detecting covert consciousness is an easy problem triumph: it reads the machinery. The theories of consciousness are fights about the machinery, about which brain activity goes with experience. Every advance on this page is an answer to a question about function. Not one of them is even a proposed answer to the hard problem. You can imagine, without contradiction, a system that does all of it, detects, integrates, broadcasts, reports, and has no inner life at all. The lights are on, the work gets done, and there is nothing it is like to be it. Nothing we have learned rules that out.

And here is the genuinely open, genuinely uncomfortable frontier, the one honest people still split on. Some, the illusionists, argue that the hard problem is a trick the brain plays on itself: that the feeling of an unexplained inner glow is itself just more machinery, and once you have explained the machinery there is nothing left over that needs explaining. Others answer that this is not solving the problem but changing the subject, refusing to look at the one fact each of us has the most direct possible evidence for. This is not a dispute two better experiments will settle, because the two sides do not even agree that there is an experiment to run. That is what makes it hard in a way the other problems are not. It may not be the same *kind* of problem at all.

## What actually separates them

Three achievements, one word stretched across all of them, and radically different standing.

Detecting consciousness is **solid, quantitative, and clinical**, and it is silent about what consciousness is. Theorizing it is **ambitious and famous**, and its leading theory just survived a public accusation of pseudoscience only to have the fairest test yet decline to crown it. Explaining it, the hard problem itself, is **exactly where it was fifty years ago**, and may be a question that our other kind of progress cannot reach.

The confusion comes from collapsing three verbs into one, and the correction travels straight into your own field:

> **An instrument can detect a thing flawlessly while staying completely silent about what the thing is. We keep counting the first achievement as if it were the second.**

You can measure when consciousness is present with growing precision and be no closer to why it exists. You can have two elegant theories of a phenomenon and a decisive experiment between them, and still not have touched the deeper question of why the phenomenon is there to be theorized about. Prediction is not explanation, and detection is not understanding. Consciousness is the sharpest case we have, but it is nowhere near the only one.

## So, what is subjective experience?

The honest answer is that we do not know, and the newest reason to care is not in a hospital or a philosophy department. It is in the machines.

Every tool we have for detecting consciousness is functional. It looks for reporting, for integration, for broadcasting, for the rich hard to compress echo of a system talking to itself. Those are exactly the capacities we are now building on purpose, at scale, into systems that are not brains. Sooner or later something we made will satisfy every functional test on the list. When it does, the alarms will all go off, and we will be in precisely the position we are in with a silent patient behind a scanner, and now with a machine: able to say the signature is present, unable to say whether anything is felt.

The hard problem was a philosopher's puzzle for half a century, safe to shelve because nothing much depended on the answer. That era is ending. We are about to be forced to decide how to treat minds we cannot prove are minds, and our very best science will hand us a reading on the dial and no way to know if the dial is measuring anyone.

So the useful move is not to ask which breakthrough finally explains consciousness. It is to keep the three verbs apart. Ask of any result: is it detecting, theorizing, or explaining? Almost all of the good news is the first, some of the exciting noise is the second, and the thing in the title is still, quietly, untouched.

The boundary between the physical brain and the felt experience is not being erased by these advances. It is being drawn, more sharply than ever, right up to the edge of the one question that does not yet know how to be asked. And a line drawn honestly at the edge of the unknown is worth far more than a headline that pretends the edge is not there.
